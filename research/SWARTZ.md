# Aaron Swartz — the record, tiered

**21 September 2026. Research, unpublished.** Asked for as *"lets look into aaron swartz"*, with the
author's correction on posture: the medical examiner's ruling is not the report's finding. The page
already runs that rule for Epstein — *manner* and *attribution* are separate evidentiary questions
with separate records, and a ruling is recorded at its tier, not adopted. This file applies the same
rule and reports what the record actually holds for each question. Sources in
`research/sources/ledger.jsonl`; the MIT Review Panel report (182 pp.) was extracted and read in full.

---

## 0. The finding in one paragraph

The death has a thin record and the prosecution has a thick one, and the two must not be swapped.
On **manner**, the record from here is a medical examiner's spokesperson, a partner who found him,
and no note: *attributed*, and — unlike Epstein — with **no custody, no camera, no cellmate, no
contested autopsy, and no pathologist retained to contest the ruling**. Nothing found bears against
the ruling, and nothing found independently confirms it. That is a null, printed. On **what preceded
it**, the record is primary and documented: a federal investigation opened **the day before anyone
was identified**; the Secret Service in the arrest party through its own task force on a call that
"in none of" the MIT Police's usual six-a-year computer calls had drawn a federal agent; **MIT's
network logs and an 87 GB packet capture handed to the Secret Service without a subpoena** and MIT's
counsel later telling the defence the opposite, which MIT's own panel calls *"not true"*; a
four-count indictment unbundled into **thirteen felonies** from the same conduct; the state case
**dismissed because the US Attorney and the Secret Service withheld discovery from the district
attorney**; a prosecutor who told MIT's counsel that "general deterrence of others" was a
consideration; plea offers of six months for guilty pleas to every count, the last one rejected
**two days before the death**; both nominal victims on record that they did not want the
prosecution; and a co-authored 2008 manifesto used as one man's statement of intent. The oversight
that followed produced letters, a briefing and a bill that died in committee; the statutory
narrowing came eight years later from the Supreme Court, which expressly left open the one question
the bill would have settled. **What the record supports is an instrument, not a mechanism:** the
same Department that declined federal charges against Epstein in 2007 brought thirteen against a
26-year-old for downloading journal articles. That is documented prosecutorial discretion running in
two directions, and it is the report's subject.

## 2. The prosecution — documented, primaries read

**Sources for this section:** MIT, *Report to the President: MIT and the Prosecution of Aaron
Swartz* (Abelson, Diamond, Grosso, Pfeiffer; 30 July 2013), read in full; DOJ/USAO-MA press release
19 July 2011; JSTOR statement 19 July 2011; H.R. 2454 and S. 1196 (113th Congress) at govinfo.

### 2.2 What the Panel found about MIT — documented, and against MIT's own account

- **Pre-subpoena production.** Items 1–4 — network flow logs, DHCP and RADIUS logs, the network
  registration records, and "the packet stream captured by the MIT network engineer … some 87
  gigabytes in all" — "were provided by MIT to the Secret Service **without a subpoena having been
  issued to MIT**." Appendix 10 concludes this was lawful under the ECPA/SCA exceptions the Panel
  applies. The lawfulness is the Panel's opinion; the fact of production is documented.
- **The false statement to the defence.** In a call with defence counsel, "the OGC attorney said
  that MIT had not turned over anything to the government without a subpoena. **In fact, this was
  not true**, as the attorney offered during his interviews with the Review Panel." The Panel
  attributes it to lapse of memory. The consequence is documented regardless: the defence spent
  until the day before the death trying to get from MIT what MIT had already handed the
  government.
- **Neutrality as practised.** "MIT never requested that a criminal prosecution be brought." MIT
  "never issued a public statement about Swartz's prosecution or advocated publicly on his behalf,
  even though doing this was urged by Aaron Swartz's family and legal team and by two members of
  the faculty." MIT "did not inquire into the details of the charges until a year after the
  indictment, and did not form an opinion about their merits." Its outside counsel told the
  prosecutor MIT "did not focus on punishment or retribution"; **the prosecutor "responded that
  the government believed that some custody was appropriate" and "had to consider … general
  deterrence of others."**
- **Weinberg's account** (defence counsel, to the *Boston Globe*, attributed): he nearly negotiated
  a no-jail plea; "JSTOR signed off on it, but MIT would not."
- **JSTOR's position, documented in its own words:** "It was the government's decision whether to
  prosecute, not JSTOR's … Once this was achieved [securing the content], we had no interest in
  this becoming an ongoing legal matter." JSTOR's counsel, Mary Jo White, is reported (AP, via
  Peters) to have called the lead prosecutor and asked him to drop the case — attributed.

**So both nominal victims are on the record as not wanting the prosecution, and the one whose
network was used says so in a report it commissioned against itself.** The prosecution ran on the
government's own account of harm — "general deterrence."

### 2.3 The charging instrument

- Four counts became thirteen from the same conduct. The MIT Panel: the superseding indictment
  "'unbundled' these counts, and created 13 counts out of the initial four. Also, the theory of
  criminality in the last count was expanded." Lofgren and Wyden (Wired, June 2013): "more than a
  third of the charges in the superseding indictment against him were under this redundant CFAA
  provision."
- Plea history (Panel, from both sides): a **six-month** offer pre-indictment, rejected; post-
  indictment offers "up to six months" as a split sentence, requiring a guilty plea to **all**
  counts; defence counsel Peters: a four-month offer at one point with the warning that later
  offers would be worse, and, two days before the death, six months with pleas to all thirteen.
- The estate's letter to DOJ, 28 January 2013 (attributed; the letter itself not obtained): AUSA
  Heymann "and [Secret Service] Agent Pickett **directed and controlled the investigation** of Mr.
  Swartz from the time of [his] arrest on January 6"; "the difference between an offer of four
  months and a threat of seven years went far beyond the minimal reduction … for 'acceptance of
  responsibility.'"
- **The manifesto as intent.** The government "pointed to a 'Guerilla Manifesto' he posted on the
  Internet in 2008." Panel, footnote 13: "**Aaron Swartz was not the sole author** of the memo, and
  it is unknown whether he authored the sentences that were quoted. Quinn Norton told the Review
  Panel that she did the final editing of the piece, and that she does not know who the other
  authors were, or who contributed which part." A co-authored document read as one man's statement
  of intent is the **naming error** in its purest form, and it is documented in the defendant's
  favour by the institution that was not on his side. Norton's own account: she told the
  investigators about it, and "in doing so … I made everything worse."
- **Motive itself: open.** "As far as the Review Panel knows, Aaron Swartz made no statement after
  his arrest regarding what he had planned to do with the downloaded documents. The Review Panel
  views the question … as remaining open."

### 2.4 The prior — PACER, 2008

Public.Resource.Org's PACER project (Malamud): ~2.7 million federal court documents pulled through
a free library trial. "The move drew the attention of the FBI, which ultimately decided not to press
charges as the documents were, in fact, public." Documented as an FBI investigation with no charges;
the FBI file (vault.fbi.gov) refuses the crawler (403), so the file's contents are not read here.
Cornyn's later question to Holder — *"Was the prosecution of Mr. Swartz in any way retaliation for
his exercise of his rights as a citizen under the Freedom of Information Act?"* — puts the link on
the record as a question. **Retaliation: not established at any tier.** The dates and the agencies
are documented; the connection is not.

**The one structural result came from the Court, not Congress.** *Van Buren v. United States*,
No. 19-783, decided 3 June 2021 (read at law.cornell.edu): "An individual 'exceeds authorized
access' when he accesses a computer with authorization but then obtains information located in
particular areas of the computer … that are off-limits to him" — a "gates-up-or-down" reading, and
the Court says plainly that on the government's reading "millions of otherwise law-abiding citizens
are criminals." **But footnote 8 leaves open exactly what Aaron's Law would have fixed:** "we need
not address whether this inquiry turns only on technological (or 'code-based') limitations on
access, or instead also looks to limits contained in contracts or policies." Aaron's Law §2 would
have defined access without authorization as "knowingly circumventing one or more technological or
physical measures." Thirteen years on, that sentence has not been enacted and the Court has
declined to reach it. The MIT Panel's own words on the statute stand: the CFAA "has the effect of
transforming minor violations of very broad service terms from a contractual issue … into a
potential federal felony."

## 4. Symmetry — what runs the other way

- The downloading was real, repeated after two blocks, and on 9 October 2010 it "brought down
  multiple JSTOR servers." The MAC address was changed to evade a block. The laptop was placed in
  a network closet. None of that is contested and the file does not soften it.
- The Panel found MIT's pre-subpoena production **lawful**, and its false statement to the defence
  a lapse rather than a lie. Those are its findings and they are recorded as such.
- The six-month offers were, on their face, lenient against a thirteen-count exposure; the
  government's position was that a felony conviction was required. Whether a felony for this
  conduct was proportionate is the question every oversight letter asked and none answered.
- The Secret Service's presence has an innocent documented mechanism — the Task Force is a Secret
  Service-organised body and the Cambridge detective who took the call was sitting in its federal
  field office that day. **Read the mechanism before reading the presence.**

Fraud and Abuse Act` **0**. `Secret Service` 3, none related. `PACER` 0. `Van Buren` 0.

The report's subject is institutional failure documented from the institution's own paper. This
case has that in unusual density: the accused institution (MIT) commissioned the report that
documents its own pre-subpoena production and its own false statement; the nominal victim (JSTOR)
published its own disinterest in prosecution; the prosecuting office's press release states the
35-year exposure in its own voice. **The keepable juxtaposition is prosecutorial discretion as the
instrument:** the same Department of Justice that, in the 2007 Non-Prosecution Agreement already
on the page, declined federal charges against Epstein, brought thirteen felony counts against
Swartz for downloading journal articles that, in the prosecuting office's own release, he "could
have accessed … for legitimate research" through his fellowship. Different
districts, different years, different facts — and the same discretion, exercised in opposite
directions, both documented. That belongs beside the NPA material or in Section XVI (the record
and its keepers), at documented tier, with the death carried exactly as §1 above carries it:
ruling attributed, custody null, cause not reached. **Author's call.**
