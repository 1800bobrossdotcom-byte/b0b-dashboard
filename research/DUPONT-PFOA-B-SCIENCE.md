## 1. The naming question: Teflon, PTFE, PFOA/C8, PFAS
- Teflon is a brand of PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene), a polymer. PFOA (C8, perfluorooctanoic acid; used as its ammonium salt, APFO) is a separate small surfactant molecule that was used as an emulsifier to make PTFE and other fluoropolymers. EPA: it "is not consumed during the polymerization process", i.e. it is a process chemical, not an ingredient of the finished polymer. PFAS is the whole class (thousands of substances); PFOA, PFOS and PTFE are all inside it.
- A "Teflon in blood" statement is a naming slip in nearly every case. What labs measure in blood (CDC NHANES, cord-blood studies, the EWG 2005 test) is PFOA, PFOS and other small PFAS, never PTFE. No source read reports PTFE (the polymer) measured in human blood. EWG's 2005 report is the origin of the loose phrasing: it calls PFOA "the Teflon chemical" and the PFC family "Active ingredients or breakdown products of Teflon". EPA's account (processing aid, not consumed) contradicts "active ingredient".

### 1c. Does PTFE itself do harm? Polymer fume fever (separate, documented, acute)
- Overheating PTFE-coated cookware can release fumes that cause "polymer fume fever" (flu-like illness). A 2015 clinical review calls it "separate and distinct" from metal fume fever and notes cookware overheating as a common exposure route. It is typically self-limited (hours to days), with rare severe cases in people with cardiorespiratory disease or in enclosed spaces. First occupational case series was 1951. It is an inhalation event from heated polymer, a different mechanism from blood PFOA.
- Thresholds differ by source: Germany's BfR names about 360 degrees C (empty pan, strongly heated); Poison Control says the coating breaks down above about 500 F (about 260 C). Treat the threshold as unsettled; both agree it needs overheating.
- Counts: Poison Control says "only a few hundred cases" reported to US poison centres in 2023 and that the figure is likely underreported (symptoms are delayed and mimic a cold). No other figure is used here (a "267" and a "3,600" circulating in search snippets were not read at source).

## 2. "Children born with Teflon in their systems": cord blood, placental transfer, milk, NHANES
- PFOA crosses the placenta. ATSDR (CDC): "Some PFAS cross the placenta and enter umbilical cord blood." Baltimore cord serum (births 26 Nov 2004 to 16 Mar 2005, Johns Hopkins Hospital, anonymised leftover samples): PFOA detected in 100% of samples, geometric mean 1.6 ng/mL (n = 299 in the ES&T paper; n = 293 in the EHP birth-size paper), PFOS 4.9 ng/mL. The Baltimore samples measured PFOA and PFOS (and 8 other PFAS), not PTFE.
- Cord levels are lower than maternal. A French cohort (about 100 pairs, 2010-13): summed 7 PFAAs median 5.70 ng/mL maternal vs 2.83 ng/mL cord. Transfer is substance-dependent; the authors conclude trans-placental passage and breast-feeding are "both significant routes".
- Breast milk carries far less than serum but adds to the infant's load: milk 20 to 150 fold lower than serum in the French study; in a Swedish cohort of 2-4-month-olds, PFOA rose "8-11% per week of exclusive breast-feeding" and the median infant/maternal PFOA ratio was 2.8. A German/Hungarian pilot (2008) found PFOA above its limit of quantitation (200 ng/L) in only 11 of 70 milk samples. So milk transfer is real and variable; one pilot barely detected PFOA.
- EWG 2005 (advocacy, with Commonweal; labs AXYS and Flett): 10 Red Cross cord-blood samples from Aug-Sept 2004; 287 chemicals total, avg 200 per baby; PFOS in all 10; PFCs "tested for 12, found 9". The press relay names "the Teflon chemical PFOA". n = 10, not a population sample. EWG's own PDF does not give a PFOA-specific count in the text read; the "10 of 10" in the PDF is attached to the PFC family block and to PFOS, not PFOA specifically.

### 2b. NHANES: how common, and the decline since the phase-out
- US population serum PFOA, geometric mean, ages 12+: 5.2 ug/L (1999-2000); 3.9 ug/L (2003-04, detected in 99.7%); 1.56 ng/mL (2015-16, ATSDR profile); 1.42 ng/mL (2017-18, 95th percentile 3.77). Computed from those two ends (my arithmetic, not a source's): 1.42 / 5.2 = 0.27, i.e. about 73% lower in 18 years. The 2007 paper attributes the early fall mainly to 3M's 2002 end of PFOS-related production; CDC today credits EPA enlisting manufacturers to phase out PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFNA. CDC's Botelho 2025: four legacy PFAS fell 16% to 87% since 1999-2000, yet in 2017-March 2020 "more than 96%" of 12-19-year-olds still had measurable levels.
- Method caution (from ATSDR): from 2013 CDC reports PFOA as the sum of linear plus branched isomers, which may affect comparison across periods. I did not quote this at source (it came from a search summary); it is a lead.

## 3. "Teflon attaching to DNA"
- Does PFOA bind DNA? Not as a mechanism the regulators or IARC rest on. EPA's 2016 PFOA assessment: "PFOA lacks the ability to react with and modify DNA", adding that its charge would "permit interaction with chromosomal histone proteins". That is a stated possibility about histones (proteins packaging DNA), not a measured binding to DNA. No paper on PFOA binding DNA turned up in a Europe PMC title/abstract search (46 hits, all PPAR/nuclear-receptor, liver, bone, etc.; none on direct DNA binding). Treat as "not found", not "disproved".
- PFOA does bind a protein: in human plasma it is "highly bound (99.7%) to albumin" (in vitro, 2003 study cited by EPA). That is the "attaches" fact that belongs to blood chemistry, and it explains the long half-life (EPA cites a half-life of 2.3 years in the general population, Bartell et al. 2010).
- Genotoxicity is mixed-to-negative: ATSDR (2021): PFOA "can produce DNA damage, but is not mutagenic at noncytotoxic concentrations"; "No studies of genotoxicity in humans exposed to perfluoroalkyls were located." EPA 2016 table: Ames/E. coli negative (one non-reproducible positive); CHO chromosome-aberration positive with activation and equivocal without; micronucleus negative. Much of the negative data is unpublished industry testing summarised by Butenhoff (2014) and Kennedy (2004) per ATSDR (sponsor identity not read; flagged).
- IARC (Monograph 135, Working Group 7-14 Nov 2023; Lancet Oncology summary 30 Nov 2023; full volume online 14 Feb 2025): PFOA Group 1, PFOS Group 2B. Stated basis for PFOA: sufficient evidence in experimental animals (a single GLP rat study, increased benign plus malignant neoplasms in both sexes) plus strong mechanistic evidence in exposed humans for epigenetic alterations and immunosuppression; human cancer evidence only "limited" (renal cell carcinoma, testis), described as "somewhat inconsistent" across studies. PFOS: Group 2B on strong mechanistic evidence alone (human evidence inadequate; animal limited). The "epigenetic alterations" are "gene-specific methylation and cancer-related miRNAs" in exposed people. The ten key characteristics IARC scored as strong are 4, 5, 7, 8, 10 (epigenetic, oxidative stress, immunosuppressive, receptor-mediated, proliferation/cell death); the Q&A's strong list does not include the genotoxicity characteristic (my reading of the absence, not an IARC sentence). So "attaching to DNA" is not IARC's basis; "altering how DNA is read (methylation)" is.

### 3b. Epigenetic (DNA methylation) associations with prenatal PFOA, cord blood and newborn blood
- Methylation is a chemical tag the body's own enzymes place on DNA. It is not PFOA attaching to DNA. Birth-cohort findings exist but are modest and not consistent across cohorts.
- Hokkaido (Japan; n = 177 pairs): higher maternal PFOA was associated with lower cord-blood IGF2 methylation; the authors' mediation analysis suggested about 21% of the PFOA-ponderal-index association ran through that methylation. One gene, one cohort.
- Healthy Start (Colorado; n = 583 pairs, 450K array): one differentially methylated site tied to PFOA; the authors say the changes may "suggest potential associations" with growth, metabolism, immune function.
- ECHO consortium (6 cohorts, n = 1017): one differentially methylated region for PFOA; PFHxS and PFOS had CpG hits. Upstate KIDS (newborn blood spots): "limited evidence" of association at high concentrations.

### 4c. Developmental effects: what the studies say, and where they diverge
- Birth weight: Baltimore (n = 293): PFOA "negatively associated with birth weight" (beta -104 g per ln-unit, 95% CI -213 to 5; the interval touches zero) and with ponderal index and head circumference; no association with length or gestational age; the authors call the associations "small". Johnson et al. 2014 (Navigation Guide systematic review, 18 human studies, 9 meta-analysed): each 1 ng/mL higher PFOA associated with -18.9 g (95% CI -29.8 to -7.9) birth weight; "sufficient" human evidence that developmental PFOA exposure reduces fetal growth. The review states reverse causality (kidney clearance in mothers of small babies) could not be ruled out: "we cannot disprove this hypothesis".
- Contrast: C8 Panel null on low birth weight (4a). Net: mixed, small effect, direction mostly negative in meta-analysis; mechanism contested.
- Vaccine response: Faroe Islands cohort (n = 587 followed): in children, a 2-fold higher major-PFC level in serum was associated with a -49% (95% CI -67% to -23%) difference in overall tetanus/diphtheria antibody concentration; PFOS strongest, PFOA also associated. ## Verdict on the author's three phrases (science beat)
1. "DuPont and Teflon poisoning": Teflon (PTFE, the polymer) is not the documented poison; PFOA (C8), the processing aid, is the substance with the health literature. Documented: PFOA in nearly all US blood; IARC Group 1 for PFOA (hazard classification, human cancer evidence limited); C8 Panel probable links (six, legal-settlement standard); NASEM sufficient-evidence outcomes (cholesterol, kidney cancer, antibody response, birth weight). Separately documented and acute: polymer fume fever from overheated PTFE (benign, self-limited in the usual case). "Poisoning" overstates the human findings as a blanket term; the findings are associations at population-exposure levels and probable-link diseases at the higher Ohio Valley exposures.
2. "Teflon attaching to DNA": unsupported as stated. PFOA binds blood albumin (documented, 99.7% in vitro). EPA: PFOA "lacks the ability to react with and modify DNA". Genotoxicity is mixed (DNA damage in some cell assays, mostly negative mutagenicity, no human genotoxicity studies located by ATSDR). What IARC cites is "epigenetic alterations" (methylation patterns), and cord-blood methylation studies show small, inconsistent associations. No source read reports PTFE or PFOA covalently bound to human DNA.
3. "Children born with Teflon in their systems": the PTFE reading is unsupported; the PFOA/PFAS reading is documented. PFOA is detected in 100% of Baltimore cord serum (geometric mean 1.6 ng/mL), crosses the placenta variably, and reaches infants in milk. Health consequence: small birth-weight associations (contested against the C8 Panel null) and lower vaccine antibody levels (a marker); the C8 Panel found no probable link to birth defects, miscarriage, preterm birth or ADHD/learning disability.
