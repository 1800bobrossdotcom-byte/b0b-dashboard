# pfoa-corporate
Beat: the DuPont / Chemours / Corteva / 3M record on PFOA (C8), read at primaries where possible. Written 24 Oct 2026. Spider-only. Dollar figures in SEC filings are in millions; the filings print them as "$ 670.7" with a space.

## 0. Naming first (what the companies' own papers say Teflon, PFOA and GenX are)
- Teflon is a fluoropolymer brand. PFOA (C8, APFO) was a processing aid used to make fluoropolymers, including some Teflon products, at Washington Works. EPA's 2005 press release, EPA's own settlements page and Chemours's filing all say it this way. GenX (HFPO dimer acid) replaced PFOA as the processing aid. No source read in this beat measured Teflon (the polymer) in a person or in water; every measurement read is of PFOA, or of other PFAS.
- The companies' denials turn on a narrower phrase than "never used". Chemours: it never used PFOA and never sold it. Corteva: EIDP "never made or sold PFOA as a commercial product". Chemours's own note says the pre-2015 DuPont performance-chemicals segment did make PFOA at Fayetteville and used it as a polymerization aid at Washington Works and four other sites. The two statements are consistent only if "commercial product" means "sold". Both are printed below.

## 2. The 1981 pregnant-worker data and what was reported to EPA
- Documented in EPA's record: a one-page DuPont document titled "C-8 BLOOD SAMPLING RESULTS", with a "Births and Pregnancies" subheading, covers eight employees. It has a column recording whether each child is "normal" or has birth defects, with birth dates from 1980 to 1981, and cord-blood C-8 values for two children. One entry reads "Normal child - born April 1981. Umbilical cord blood 0.055 ppm." EPA treated this as the first human evidence of PFOA crossing the placenta. DuPont admitted the document contains numbers purporting to be PFOA in employees' blood.
- What the EPA record does NOT give: how many of the children were recorded as having birth defects, or what defects. The count and the defect type reach this note only as an allegation by the New York Attorney General (9 July 2026 press release): "two of the seven pregnant workers ... had babies with eye and nostril defects", with DuPont said to have abandoned the study. I could not read the underlying document or any DuPont reply to that allegation.
- Reporting to EPA: EPA first saw the 1981 data when an outside attorney (Robert A. Bilott, a named party in the record) sent it on 6 March 2001; EPA says DuPont had not submitted it under TSCA §8(e), and that DuPont's 6 June 1997 reply to an EPA request for "known toxicological information" omitted it.
- DuPont's account: it argued the 1981 data was "merely confirmatory" of animal data it had already given EPA, and its retained toxicologist's declaration, quoted by the judge, says the cord-blood datum was "neither toxicological nor informative". The judge did not decide the question; she sent it to hearing, which the settlement then replaced.
- 3M side of 1981 (state trial exhibit PTX1266, Minnesota v. 3M, a 3M internal note of a DuPont phone call of 14 Dec 1981): 3M had seen "birth defects in the eyes of unborn rats" in a preliminary study and had excluded female employees of childbearing capability from C-8 areas; by December the further studies were read as showing the effect was probably a tissue-preparation artefact: "it does not seem that the observed effects on the eyes of the unborn rats were due to C-8". The note says nothing about human babies or placental transfer. "3M's placental-transfer finding" (brief wording) is therefore NOT supported by anything read; the rat eye finding is documented, was walked back in the same document, and is a rat finding.

## 3. EPA TSCA §8(e) / RCRA case: complaint (2004) and settlement (2005)
- EPA filed two administrative complaints, 8 July 2004 (amended 13 Oct 2004) and 6 Dec 2004. The settlement forwarded to the Environmental Appeals Board on 14 Dec 2005 resolved those and four more counts found in 41 boxes DuPont gave EPA in Dec 2004-Jan 2005: eight counts, seven under TSCA §8(e) and one RCRA permit count.
- Counts: (1) the 1981 transplacental data; (2) drinking-water contamination inside homes (DuPont internal guideline 1 ppb, exceeded from the 1980s); (3) RCRA, the 1997 information request; (4) Sept 2004, blood PFOA 15.7 to 128 ppb in twelve people near the plant; (5) a 2002 blood test of ten residents; (6-8) three 1997 rat inhalation studies of other perfluorinated chemicals (identities claimed as confidential business information).
- Terms: $10,250,000 civil penalty (not tax-deductible) plus two supplemental environmental projects worth $6,250,000 ($5m fluorotelomer-to-PFOA biodegradation testing, later amended: $1.5m redirected to university analytical instruments in 2013; $1.25m microscale chemistry in seven Wood County schools). EPA called it the largest civil administrative penalty it had obtained under any statute, and "more than ten times" the previous largest §8(e) penalty.
- EPA's own arithmetic: the theoretical statutory maximum for Count 1 alone was $183,837,500 (1981 to 2001 at per-day maxima); the board memo says EPA settled far below that for litigation risk (statute of limitations; whether the omission "directly disrupted" EPA; the 1991 Compliance Audit Program waiver for Count 2).
- No admission, verbatim from the signed consent agreement: DuPont "neither admits nor denies the factual allegations, and denies the legal allegations"; "Nothing in this Consent Agreement should be taken as an admission of liability". DuPont, in the agreement, "denies these allegations".
- Follow-on: EPA's SDWA orders at Washington Works tightened the PFOA action level from 150 ppb (2002) to 0.50 ppb (2006), 0.40 ppb (2009) and 70 ppt (2017 amendment); EPA says each was based partly on information gathered in the TSCA/RCRA settlement.

## 4. Tennant (1998-2001) and Leach v. DuPont (filed 2001; settled 2004-05)
- Tennant: attributed only. The Intercept / Investigative Fund (2015) reports a Parkersburg cattle farmer brought his dying herd to an attorney in 1999 (the brief says 1998-99; the outlet says 1999), that the case discovery produced DuPont's internal C8 records, and that "In 2001, DuPont quickly settled the Tennant case for an undisclosed sum." The same piece says DuPont estimated C8 in a stream cattle drank from at 100 ppb in 1991, 100 times its internal drinking-water guideline. I could not read a court record, a settlement or any DuPont statement specific to Tennant.
- Leach: class action filed August 2001 (AP) on behalf of residents of six water districts near Washington Works. Agreement in principle announced 9 Sept 2004, approved by the Wood County Circuit Court on 28 Feb 2005. DuPont's own press release gave the terms: cash and expenditures "valued at $85 million" plus $22.6m legal fees; $70m initial payment ($20m to health and education projects); water treatment for six water districts (est. $10m); a $5m independent community study by a science panel; and, only if the panel found a "probable link" to any disease, medical monitoring "up to $235 million", with DuPont then not contesting general causation in personal-injury suits. If no link were found, personal-injury claims would be released. AP put the total cost at $107.6m minimum.
- The judge approved it "without any evidence" that PFOA caused any disease (AP's paraphrase). DuPont's general counsel: settling "in no way implies any admission of liability". Class counsel (Bilott) said the deal preserved personal-injury rights if a link was found.
- C8 Health Project (Aug 2005-Jul 2006): blood samples from "about 69,000" people, run by an independent company. The class is "about 80,000" per Chemours/Corteva.
- C8 Science Panel (three epidemiologists chosen jointly by the parties; Fletcher, Savitz, Steenland; 2005-2013): the definition of "probable link" is legal, "more likely than not". Six probable links, filed with the court in four batches (Dec 2011; Apr 2012; Jul 2012; Oct 2012): diagnosed high cholesterol, ulcerative colitis, thyroid disease, testicular cancer, kidney cancer, pregnancy-induced hypertension (incl. preeclampsia).
- NO probable link found (print as nulls): birth defects; miscarriage and stillbirth; preterm birth and low birth weight; neurodevelopmental disorders in children (ADHD, learning disabilities); type 2 diabetes; coronary artery disease; diagnosed hypertension; stroke; chronic kidney disease; liver disease; asthma and COPD; osteoarthritis; Parkinson's disease; common infections incl. influenza; and the autoimmune diseases other than ulcerative colitis (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, type 1 diabetes, Crohn's, MS); all cancers other than kidney and testicular. Alzheimer's and ALS "could not be assessed". On birth defects the panel said limited evidence for congenital heart defects in one study "was considered most likely to be due to chance".
- Status of monitoring: DuPont must fund up to $235m; about $2m disbursed since 2012 (Corteva, 30 Jun 2026), with about $1m left in the account; in Sept 2025 the medical panel directed changes so monitoring continues for kidney and testicular cancer and ulcerative colitis (Chemours 10-K). The filing does not say whether monitoring for the other three diseases was reduced; unresolved.
- Contested: a 2025 review in Frontiers in Public Health argues newer epidemiology "call[s] into question" the kidney-cancer and thyroid-disease links. Its authors work for a consulting firm that discloses serving corporations and law firms, one author has been retained as a defence expert in PFAS litigation, and partial funding came from a law firm. The Panel members' own 2020 review (PubMed 32950793) was refused to the spider (HTTP 203) and is unread; a search-engine summary said they consider kidney and testicular evidence "supportive but not definitive" - NOT used as a finding.

## 5. The MDL (S.D. Ohio, No. 2:13-md-2433): verdicts, 2017 settlement, 2021 and 2024 follow-ons; spin-off; cost-sharing MOU
- After the Panel's findings, Chemours says about 3,500 suits were filed in federal and state courts in Ohio and West Virginia and consolidated in the Ohio MDL (docket 2:13-md-2433, confirmed on CourtListener). The six disease categories above were the only ones the Leach settlement let class members sue on.
- Verdicts (Chemours's 10-K FY2016; settlement wording from later filings): Bartlett (kidney cancer), Oct 2015: $1.1m negligence + $0.5m emotional distress, no punitive damages. Freeman (testicular cancer), Jul 2016: $5.1m compensatory + $0.5m punitive, with plaintiff's counsel claiming at least $6.9m fees and costs. Vigneron (testicular cancer), Nov 2016: $2m compensatory + $10.5m punitive plus fees. Three more bellwethers settled before trial "for amounts well below the incremental cost of preparing for trials"; one was removed because the plaintiff "did not suffer from the alleged disease". 
- 11 Feb 2017: agreement in principle to settle every MDL case and claim, including cases tried to verdict, for $670.7m cash, half paid by Chemours and half by DuPont. The filing says it was "not in any way an admission of liability or fault".
- Second settlement, Jan 2021: about $83m for 96 post-2017 cases (Chemours ~$29m; DuPont and Corteva ~$27m each). One testicular-cancer case went to trial in March 2020: $40m compensatory/emotional-distress + $10m loss of consortium; no punitive damages; consortium cut to $0.25m in March 2021; paid by Nov 2023 after appeals through the U.S. Supreme Court were denied. (A Sixth Circuit decision of 5 Dec 2022, appeal 21-3418, is in CourtListener's index; I did not read its text, and I am inferring, not reading, that it is this case.)
- Third, Nov-Dec 2024: $58.5m for 45 filed cases and 29 pre-suit claims, before the first 2024 trial; paid in two instalments (last March 2025); MDL closed by the JPML in Feb 2025. Chemours's and DuPont's text rounds it to $59m. Future personal-injury claims go to the courts where they are filed.
- Spin-off: Chemours became independent on 1 July 2015 (EPA/DOJ complaint). Chemours says "Many actions include an allegation of fraudulent transfer in the spin-off" and that it declined EID's indemnity requests on those claims.
- Cost-sharing MOU (22 Jan 2021; DuPont, Corteva, EIDP, Chemours): legacy PFAS costs from pre-1 July 2015 conduct shared 50% Chemours / 50% DuPont+Corteva until the earlier of 31 Dec 2040 or $4,000m of "Qualified Spend"; DuPont+Corteva's half capped at $2bn; an escrow (target $700m by 2028). DuPont's 30 Jun 2026 10-Q: it had paid about $745m of its portion. Chemours says about $1.3bn remained available after the Ohio, Delaware and New Jersey payments (FY2025 10-K).

## 7. PFOA Stewardship Program (2006) and GenX at Fayetteville (Cape Fear)
- EPA launched the programme in January 2006 and asked eight companies to cut PFOA from facility emissions and product content by 95% no later than 2010 and work toward elimination no later than 2015. DuPont and 3M/Dyneon were among them. Final reports were submitted early 2016; "All participating companies state that they met the PFOA Stewardship Program goals" (a company statement, not an EPA audit); EPA adds that PFOA "may be produced, imported, and used by companies not participating".
- GenX (HFPO-DA) replaced PFOA at Washington Works (EPA's settlements page). On 28 Jan 2009 EPA and DuPont entered a TSCA consent order governing GenX manufacture; the order is quoted in North Carolina's 2018 draft injunction as saying EPA had concerns that it "will persist in the environment, could bioaccumulate, and be toxic" and requiring "recover and capture (destroy) or recycle ... at an overall efficiency of 99%". (That text is a State of North Carolina filing quoting the order, not the order.) The 2026 federal decree again requires compliance with the "existing TSCA GenX 5(e) Order".
- Fayetteville Works: Chemours says it has operated the site since 2015 and that "HFPO Dimer Acid is manufactured at Fayetteville." WUNC (24 Jun 2026) reports that Chemours employees "in 2017 told North Carolina officials" they and DuPont employees had discharged PFAS into the Cape Fear River from as early as 1980 - an outlet's report of an account, not a document read. Feb 2019 state consent order (Bladen County): Chemours paid $13m (penalty and investigative costs), a thermal oxidizer (99.99%), 75% cut in surface-water PFAS loading, replacement water supplies. Chemours's Fayetteville remediation accrual: $320m at 31 Dec 2025.
- Chemours's position: "While the Company believes that discharges from Fayetteville ... have not impacted the safety of drinking water in North Carolina". In Feb 2019 EPA sent a TSCA notice of violation, which Chemours answered asserting it "has not violated environmental laws"; that NOV was folded into the June 2026 decree.
- Regulatory status of the two chemicals in drinking water (Federal Register API): the 26 Apr 2024 final rule set MCLs of 4.0 ng/L for PFOA and PFOS and 10 ng/L for HFPO-DA (GenX). On 20 May 2026 EPA proposed (a) rescinding the HFPO-DA/PFHxS/PFNA/hazard-index limits, on procedural grounds, and (b) extending the PFOA/PFOS compliance date from 26 Apr 2029 to 26 Apr 2031 for systems that request it. Comments closed 20 July 2026; no final action appears in the Federal Register API as of 24 Oct 2026. PFOA's and PFOS's 4 ppt limits are not proposed for rescission.

## Verdict on the author's three phrases (corporate beat only)
1. "DuPont and Teflon poisoning": the documented core is PFOA, not Teflon. DuPont used PFOA as a processing aid at Washington Works from 1951, released it to air, ponds, landfills and the Ohio River (DuPont's own admission as recited in the EPA record), contaminated six water districts, was fined $10.25m by EPA in 2005 for not reporting risk information (no admission), with Chemours (and Corteva, for 2021 and 2024) settled personal-injury cases for $670.7m (2017, split 50/50), about $83m (2021) and $58.5m (2024), and funded $1.185bn for water systems, and is now paying state settlements (Ohio $110m, Delaware $50m, New Jersey $875m over 25 years; NC and NY pending). A court-appointed-by-agreement panel found "probable links" (a legal standard) to six diseases and no probable link to many others. "Poisoning" of individuals is a causal claim; the companies deny it; at documented tier the record shows exposure, non-reporting and settlements, with causation established only to the "more likely than not" standard of the Leach agreement for six diseases. Whether the panel's links survive later epidemiology is contested (item 4).
2. "Teflon attaching to DNA": nothing in this beat touches DNA. No filing, EPA record, court document or panel report read here says PFOA, PTFE or any PFAS attaches to DNA. Unsupported by the sources read; the biology belongs to the other researcher.
3. "Children being born with Teflon in their systems": at this beat's evidence the supportable form is narrower and different: PFOA (not Teflon) was found in the cord blood of at least two infants in a 1981 DuPont workplace table (value 0.055 ppm recorded for a child recorded as "normal"), which EPA treated as the first human evidence of placental transfer. A New York AG allegation (untested) says two of seven pregnancies in that group produced babies with eye and nostril defects. Against it, the C8 Science Panel, with 69,000 people's data, found no probable link between PFOA and birth defects, miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, low birth weight or neurodevelopmental disorders, and a probable link only to pregnancy-induced hypertension. A 1981 rat finding of eye defects was recorded by 3M and walked back as a laboratory artefact in the same document. No source read here measures "Teflon" (PTFE) in a baby.
