HEADLINE: Art is the vault and the costume - capital hides in it, and intelligence wears it.
BODY:
<p>The Senate's own investigators called art "the largest legal, unregulated market in the United States." No Bank Secrecy Act duties at auction, shell companies as buyers, advisers for undisclosed principals, so the owner of a nine-figure object never has to be recorded anywhere. Their worked example is a sanctions case: more than $18M in art bought through a Moscow adviser after OFAC sanctions took effect. Add the Geneva Freeport's roughly 1.2 million stored works, and Salvator Mundi, sold for $450.3M to a proxy for Saudi Arabia and unseen since. This is not a market that leaks money. It is a vault for what cannot sit in a bank, built that way in public.</p>
<p>The CIA proved the other use. The Congress for Cultural Freedom ran in 35 countries; Encounter, Preuves and Der Monat ran on Agency money; MoMA's International Council toured Abstract Expressionism on secret funding. Four of the museum's mid-century leaders held intelligence portfolios. Braden went from MoMA's executive secretary to running the CIA division that paid for it all. Burden ran MoMA and sat on the Farfield Foundation, the Agency's arts conduit. In those decades the museum board was an intelligence seat.</p>
<p>On Little Saint James the central building copies a fifteenth-century bathhouse beneath a citadel, and one room is hung with masks of older men. That is staging, built to act on whoever stands inside. Money with no owner on paper, culture run as a state operation, rooms built to work on people: one seam, three uses.</p>
